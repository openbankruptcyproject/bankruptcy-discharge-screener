Do I Need a Lawyer for Bankruptcy?

You have the right to file without one. But court data shows represented debtors have significantly better outcomes.

The legal right to file pro se

Under federal law, any individual can file a bankruptcy petition without an attorney. This is called filing "pro se" (representing yourself). The court filing fees are the same whether or not you have an attorney: $338 for Chapter 7, $313 for Chapter 13.

However, the right to file pro se does not mean it is advisable in all situations.

What the data shows

According to federal court records across 94 districts, pro se bankruptcy cases have notably different outcomes than cases with attorney representation:

Pro se outcomes

Chapter 13: Pro se cases have substantially higher dismissal rates than represented cases. The procedural complexity of Chapter 13 -- monthly payments, plan confirmation, annual reporting, and trustee interactions over 3-5 years -- makes successful completion without an attorney extremely difficult.

Chapter 7: Pro se success rates are better than Chapter 13 but still lower than represented cases. Common pro se errors include improper exemption claims, failure to attend the 341 meeting, and incomplete schedules.

When you may be able to file without a lawyer

  • Simple Chapter 7 cases with few assets, straightforward debts, and no complications
  • No real property (no house, no land)
  • No business debts
  • No prior filings that create discharge bar issues
  • No creditor disputes anticipated

When you almost certainly need a lawyer

  • Chapter 13 cases -- The 3-5 year plan, confirmation process, and ongoing reporting make pro se completion extremely difficult
  • Homeowners -- Protecting a home involves exemptions, lien avoidance, and mortgage arrears curing
  • Business owners -- Business assets, debts, and tax obligations add significant complexity
  • Prior bankruptcy filings -- Discharge bar analysis requires knowledge of Sections 1328(f), 727(a)(8), and 109(g)
  • Non-dischargeable debt disputes -- Creditor challenges under Section 523
  • Tax debts -- Determining which taxes are dischargeable requires technical analysis

Free and low-cost legal help

  • Legal Aid -- Many legal aid organizations provide free bankruptcy representation to low-income individuals. Search at lawhelp.org
  • Law school clinics -- Some law schools operate bankruptcy clinics where supervised students handle cases at no cost
  • Pro bono programs -- Bar associations maintain lists of attorneys who take cases for free
  • Bankruptcy petition preparers -- Non-attorneys who can help you fill out forms (but cannot give legal advice) for $150-$300
Petition preparers are not lawyers

Bankruptcy petition preparers (BPPs) can type your information onto the official forms, but they cannot advise you on which chapter to file, which exemptions to claim, or how to handle creditor objections. Under Section 110, they face penalties for providing legal advice. If your case has any complexity, a BPP is not a substitute for an attorney.

What a bankruptcy attorney does

  • Analyzes your financial situation and recommends the appropriate chapter
  • Checks your prior filing history for discharge bars
  • Prepares and files all required documents
  • Claims proper exemptions to protect your property
  • Represents you at the 341 meeting and any court hearings
  • Handles creditor objections and adversary proceedings
  • In Chapter 13, ensures plan feasibility and manages modifications
